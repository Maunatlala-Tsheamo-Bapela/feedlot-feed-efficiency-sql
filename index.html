Efficient steers eat 1.8 kg less feed a day for the same gain

Which animals turn feed into weight most cheaply, and what is that worth in rand? An analysis of 150 feedlot steers.

1. The question

Which feed-efficiency differences drive Average Daily Gain (ADG), Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) and feed cost per kilogram of weight gained? Beef feedlots spend most of their costs on feed, so a small efficiency gap per animal adds up across a pen.

2. Data and method

The data are 150 steers from three published feedlot trials (Beck 2023, Proctor 2024, Foote 2024), with individual dry matter intake (DMI), weights, residual feed intake (RFI) and carcass results. I loaded them into PostgreSQL and calculated:

  • ADG = (final weight − initial weight) ÷ days on feed (days derived from the data)
  • FCR = DMI ÷ ADG (kg dry matter per kg gain; lower is better)
  • Feed cost per kg gain = FCR × ration cost per kg dry matter
  • Efficiency groups: steers ranked by RFI within each trial and split into thirds with SQL’s NTILE, so trial differences do not distort the comparison

Carcass weights were the same, so the efficient steers were not smaller at slaughter. The pattern held in all three trials.

4. Treatments made little difference

Within each trial, the treatment groups were close. In Beck 2023, FCR was 6.92 (control), 7.37 (10MB) and 7.50 (20MB). In Proctor 2024 it was 5.52 (high), 5.64 (neutral) and 5.91 (low). With 14 to 18 steers per group and no significance test run in SQL, I treat this as no clear treatment effect. The larger signal was the variation between individual animals.

5. What it means

  • Feed efficiency is a bigger lever than the treatments tested here: same gain and carcass, about R670 more income over feed per animal at base prices.
  • Efficiency matters most when feed is expensive. At the high feed price the least efficient third lost R465 per animal over feed while the most efficient earned R331.
  • Recording individual feed intake, or selecting for lower RFI, is the practical action. Which steers are efficient cannot be seen on arrival, so this is a case for measuring, not a forecast.
